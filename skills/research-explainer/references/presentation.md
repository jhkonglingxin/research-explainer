# Audience, format, and accessible visuals

Select a useful presentation; do not require the user to fill out a questionnaire or apply every template.

## Audience and depth

| Audience or purpose | Emphasize | Keep available |
|---|---|---|
| Interested general reader | The question, concrete finding, why it matters, central limitation | Essential numbers, source identity, terms needed to follow the claim |
| Student or new researcher | Terms, method logic, examples, how the evidence supports the conclusion | Definitions, assumptions, methodological detail needed to learn |
| Researcher from another field | Domain-specific concepts, comparison, methods, applicability | Effect measures, uncertainty, design, relevant models and assumptions |
| Team briefing or public summary | What is known, what is uncertain, what decision the evidence can inform | Context and limits needed to avoid an overstated recommendation |

Use the requested language, not the language of this skill. Preserve important original-language terms alongside translations when helpful. Do not assume that a person wants a lower reading level because of age or disability. Ask about specific access needs only when relevant.

## Useful output patterns

- **Quick answer:** main point, one supporting fact, one essential qualifier. No compulsory headings.
- **Paper explanation:** research question → study or argument → main result → interpretation and limits. Use headings only if they help.
- **Method or equation:** the problem it solves → core idea → meaning of terms or symbols → when it works and fails → a labeled example if useful.
- **Detailed report:** plain-language overview with optional technical notes, provenance, and a compact glossary. Follow the supplied report template if one exists.
- **Presentation or spoken explanation:** one message per visual, a readable short caption, and supporting details in notes. A slide is not a place to repeat the whole methods section.

Do not force a recommendations section when evidence only supports explanation. If next steps are requested, identify them as proposals rather than results.

## Figures and tables

Give enough context to answer “How was this obtained?”, “How do I read it?”, and “What can I conclude?” A caption plus one interpretation sentence can be sufficient.

For quantitative visuals, check visible axes, units, legend, reference groups, scales, uncertainty, sample or material, and whether values are absolute, normalized, adjusted, or modeled. Do not infer an error bar's meaning if it is unlabeled. Explain a truncated or logarithmic scale when it changes how the pattern looks.

For conceptual diagrams, distinguish the illustrated relationship, a proposed mechanism, a proven dependency, and the direction of information flow. An arrow does not establish causation. For qualitative displays, explain the categories and context; do not invent frequencies.

When creating or adapting a requested visual:

- Give it a descriptive title that states the comparison without exaggerating the conclusion.
- Use direct labels and units. Do not rely on color alone for meaning; use text, position, shape, or line style where helpful.
- Supply concise alt text or a text equivalent that includes the main relationship and material uncertainty. Do not claim a file is accessible without checking the requested output format.
- Respect any requested template. Use the host's suitable artifact tools if present; otherwise deliver useful text rather than pretending to have rendered a document or chart.

## Worked example: multiple testing

Synthetic source: In a laboratory comparison of 12 independently prepared samples, method B increased a measured output by 8 units relative to A. The nominal P value was 0.03; after testing 20 outputs, the reported adjusted Q value was 0.18, against Q < 0.05.

Faithful plain-language version: “Method B gave an output 8 units higher in this experiment. That difference did not meet the study's criterion after accounting for the many outputs tested, so the finding remains uncertain.” A detailed note can retain N = 12, P = 0.03, Q = 0.18, the threshold, and the source location.

Avoid: “Method B significantly improves performance.” That drops the correction and extends a specific output to general performance.

This is an invented teaching example, not a real study or a universal wording template.
