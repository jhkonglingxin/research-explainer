# Match the explanation to the evidence

Use the sections that fit the supplied material. These are interpretation checks, not a requirement to audit every paper or run a new analysis. Domain names do not determine evidence strength; identify the actual design and claim.

## Quantitative and observational research

- Explain what was measured, in whom or what, against which reference, and over what interval. Keep enrolled samples, analyzed samples, independent units, and repeat measurements distinct. Cells, technical replicates, repeated scans, or many papers from one cohort do not create the same number of independent participants or studies.
- Distinguish an association from a change over time or an effect of intervention. Statistical adjustment attempts to account for measured differences; it does not remove all confounding or make an observational result causal.
- Keep effect size and uncertainty together. A P value is not the probability that the hypothesis is true or the probability that chance caused the result. An interval spanning a null value does not establish equivalence or absence of an effect; a small P value does not establish a large or useful effect.
- Preserve the reported testing threshold and whether multiplicity was considered. Separate nominal P values from adjusted P/Q values; do not transfer a test result to another outcome, subgroup, model, or time point. Missing P values are not non-significant results.
- Relative risk, odds ratio, hazard ratio, risk difference, standardized differences, and regression coefficients are different quantities. An odds ratio of 0.70 describes odds, not a demonstrated 30% lower probability. A hazard ratio is not a cumulative-risk ratio. If baseline risk, follow-up, or the appropriate conversion is unavailable, do not invent an absolute effect or “people helped.”
- State percentage points versus relative percent change. Label any illustrative calculation and its assumptions. Keep signs tied to the scale: a higher score may mean either better or worse status.
- Preserve null, adverse, heterogeneous, and contradictory findings that affect the answer. Do not present one significant subgroup as the whole study. A measured biomarker, proxy, or correlation is not automatically a mechanism or intervention target.

## Experiments and engineering measurements

- Distinguish assignment or manipulation from measured responses. Explain controls, independent experimental units, repeats, and the conditions under which a comparison was made.
- Keep experimental accuracy, calibration error, uncertainty, instrument resolution, and model approximation separate when the source distinguishes them. Do not manufacture an uncertainty bound from a plot or from repeat counts.
- Describe practical scale using the reported units, conditions, and tolerances. A laboratory improvement does not by itself establish field durability, safety, manufacturability, or cost effectiveness.
- Separate evidence that a system responds to an intervention from evidence for the proposed mechanism. Mention randomization, masking, or controls only when provided; say when the supplied material does not establish them.

## Computational research, AI, and simulation

- Identify whether the result comes from training data, validation, a held-out test, an external dataset, a benchmark, or simulated data. Do not call a simulated result an observed real-world effect.
- Explain the metric and comparison, including what higher or lower values mean. Accuracy alone does not reveal class-specific errors, calibration, generalization, or utility. Use only metrics actually provided.
- Keep benchmark gains tied to dataset, split, baseline, settings, and uncertainty. Report a change from 89% to 92% as 3 percentage points; a relative change is a different calculation and needs a clear denominator.
- Distinguish predictive performance from causal understanding. An ablation can support a component's contribution within a system; it does not by itself prove a biological or social mechanism.
- State the assumptions and conditions of a simulation. Sensitivity analysis explores how results change under specified choices; it does not validate all those choices against reality.

## Qualitative, historical, and interpretive research

- Explain the research question, material or participants, how they were selected, analytic approach, and context when provided. Preserve participants' perspectives and distinctions among themes.
- A theme or quotation is not a population prevalence estimate. “7 of 18 interviewees mentioned X” may describe this sample if reported; purposive sampling does not support “39% of all people believe X.” Do not invent saturation, coder agreement, representative sampling, or a P value.
- Retain important counterexamples and differing interpretations. Do not reduce nuanced findings to a majority vote. Separate the source's interpretations from your own explanatory framing.
- For historical or textual arguments, distinguish primary evidence, source provenance, contextual reading, competing interpretations, and gaps. Explain how an argument is supported without treating one interpretation as settled empirical fact.
- Preserve quotation wording and attribution. If a quotation needs simplification, present a clearly labeled paraphrase rather than altered words inside quotation marks. Do not add identifying participant details.

## Theoretical and mathematical work

- Identify definitions, assumptions, the claim, and whether the source provides a proof, derivation, conjecture, heuristic, or example.
- Explain symbols and the intuition for key steps at the requested level. A simple example illustrates a theorem; it does not prove it. A numerical match does not establish a general identity.
- Keep scope conditions: domains, dimensions, boundary conditions, independence, smoothness, limits, or other assumptions that make the result true. Do not extend a result beyond them merely to simplify it.
- Distinguish a proof of a model property from evidence that the model describes the world. An intuitive sketch is not a complete proof; label it accordingly. Do not add statistical significance to work that does not use statistical inference.

## Reviews and mixed evidence

- Distinguish a narrative synthesis, systematic review, pooled estimate, and original study. Article count is not automatically independent-study count.
- Preserve eligibility, heterogeneity, uncertainty, and the study limitations that affect the conclusion when provided. Do not imply that pooling weak or overlapping evidence removes its limitations.
- For mixed methods or several evidence types, explain each contribution before integrating them. Agreement can strengthen a particular inference; disagreement is informative and should not be hidden.
- Describe absent evidence with the actual coverage: “not supplied,” “not retrieved,” “not measured,” “not reported,” and “no clear effect detected” are not interchangeable.
